## Abstract/executive summary

This report explores how gamification impacts organizational culture. Gamification incorporates game elements such as points, rewards, challenges, and competition into recruitment, training, and daily work. The example in Case F shows that gamification can make work more fun, increase employee engagement and productivity, but poorly designed gamification can also lead to stress, unfairness, and excessive competition.

## Introduction

### 1.1 Case F Background: Gamification in Modern Management

#### 1.1.1 Concept and History of Gamification

Gamification refers to “the use of game design elements in non-game contexts” (Deterding et al., 2011). It applies elements such as points, badges, challenges and rewards to encourage participation and influence behaviour. Nick Pelling is widely credited with creating the term in 2002, although its earliest documented industry use was in 2008 and it became widely adopted after 2010 (Sayeed, 2026; Deterding et al., 2011).

#### 1.1.2 Rise in Modern Management

Digital technology has allowed gamification to expand into recruitment, training, performance management and customer engagement. The global gamification market was estimated at US$34.43 billion in 2026, reflecting growing business interest (Review42 Editorial, 2026). Case F includes examples such as Unilever using games in recruitment, IBM supporting remote employees and SAP rewarding sales and environmental activities (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026).

#### 1.1.3 Motivation and Surveillance

Gamification can increase motivation by providing goals, feedback and rewards. However, performance tracking and leaderboards may also create pressure and excessive monitoring. Disney employees described their productivity-tracking system as an “electronic whip,” showing that gamification can motivate employees or become a form of control depending on its design (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026).

### 1.2 Key Engineering Management Issues

The main issue is aligning game objectives with organisational goals. Employees may focus on earning points instead of work quality or teamwork if rewards are poorly designed. Managers must also balance performance monitoring with employee autonomy, fairness and well-being.Gamification should therefore be evaluated through work quality, engagement, teamwork and employee satisfaction, rather than productivity alone. It should support effective management and organisational culture instead of replacing good management.

### Question 1

Analyze how gamification could shape an organisation’s culture. Explain which type(s) of culture may be strengthened, and why? Using evidence from the case.

Gamification may strengthen enterprising and social cultures by encouraging behaviours that become shared expectations at work. Organisational culture consists of shared beliefs and values that guide behaviour [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. When games regularly reward certain behaviours or make them part of everyday work, they show employees what the organisation values and how it expects them to act.

Gamification can strengthen an enterprising culture by encouraging competition and creative problem solving. Both are key features of an enterprising culture [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. Case F explains that rewards and competition can make routine tasks more interesting, while solving problems in games can help employees become more creative and persistent [Case F, p. 2]. Regular challenges and recognition for achievements may encourage employees to look for better solutions and improve their performance. Over time, creativity and achievement may become shared workplace values.

Gamification can also strengthen a social culture by giving colleagues more chances to interact. Social culture emphasises collaboration and trust [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. IBM uses games to help employees who work from home or travel connect with colleagues and stay engaged [Case F, p. 2]. These interactions may help employees build relationships and feel part of the organisation, making it easier for them to work together and support one another. Whether trust or collaboration increased remains unclear from the case.

Gamification can also reinforce values such as learning and social responsibility. Marriott and Siemens use games for employee training [Case F, p. 1]. Using games for training may help employees see learning as a regular part of their work. SAP awards points for environmental activities such as carpooling [Case F, p. 2]. Awarding points for these activities shows employees that the organisation values environmental responsibility.

Gamification may weaken a social culture when employees feel pressured to compete. Disney's laundry workers felt pushed to compete even when they did not want to and described productivity tracking as an "electronic whip" [Case F, p. 3]. This pressure may weaken trust in management and lead employees to put individual results ahead of working together.

### Question 2

The organisational culture iceberg model differentiates between core culture and observable culture [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, pp. 8–9]. Core culture encompasses the values, beliefs, and assumptions that dictate behavior, while observable culture encompasses visible activities and symbols. Consequently, gamification practices can offer insight into the priorities of an organisation. Nevertheless, a single practice does not guaranty that all members share the associated values. The subsequent links are interpretations of the evidence in Case F, rather than verified statements of the core values of each organisation.

The practices that are manifested above are detailed in Case F. The lecture's distinction between observable and core culture informs analytical interpretations of their connections to underlying values. These examples demonstrate the potential for gamification to both reflect and reinforce organisational priorities. SAP's points acknowledge environmentally responsible behavior, whereas Unilever's assessment criteria specify which competencies are valued. IBM's shared activities offer an alternative mechanism: opportunities for interaction may foster relationships rather than individual competition. Consequently, cultural messages are conveyed through the activities and assessments that organisations provide, in addition to the rewards they offer. Disney emphasises the significance of distinguishing between intended priorities and employee experience. A productivity-orientated system may be perceived as a source of pressure rather than motivation. This does not establish that the organisation as a whole lacks trust or respect; rather, it suggests a potential conflict between the system's priorities and the well-being of its employees. In general, gamification is only conducive to cultural alignment when its regulations, acknowledged behaviors, and employee experience are in accordance with the organisation's objectives.

### Question 3

#### 3.1 Workplace Example: Advantages and Disadvantages

Unilever used short games to assess problem-solving skills and decide which graduate applicants should move to the next stage [Case F, p. 1] (Stephan & Erickson, 2017, "Lessons from the front lines"). When games are used with existing staff, their design can affect how colleagues work together.

#### 3.2 Positive Impacts

IBM used games to help remote and travelling staff stay in touch with colleagues [Case F, p. 2]. The extra contact could help staff feel part of the team and make it easier to ask for help. This could support a social culture, which emphasises collaboration and trust [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4].

#### 3.3 Negative Impacts

Disney tracked the individual output of laundry workers, who felt pressured to keep up with one another [Case F, p. 3]. A worker may be less willing to stop and help a colleague if it means falling behind. This could weaken the trust that supports a social culture [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4].

#### 3.4 Conclusion

IBM used games to make it easier for colleagues to interact, while Disney's individual output tracking put pressure on workers to compete. If an organisation wants employees to help each other in everyday work, the game should give them chances to interact and make it worthwhile to cooperate.

### Question 4

Select ONE organisation (Unilever, SAP, Disney or equivalent) and investigate its use of gamification. Evaluate its effectiveness in terms of its purpose, design, implementation, and outcomes, using evidence from multiple external sources.

Google

Google introduced gThanks to make it easy for employees to thank colleagues for work that managers might miss (Bock, 2015). Peer recognition also gave Google a way to encourage staff to share knowledge across teams (Chen & Barolak, 2020).

**Design.** Visible Kudos worked as symbolic awards, while Peer Bonuses offered a higher level of recognition. The public awards and levels of recognition give gThanks game-like features in a work setting, consistent with Deterding et al.'s (2011) definition of gamification. Herzberg's two-factor theory treats recognition as a satisfier that can increase job satisfaction and motivation [ENG3003_L8_Ch 16 Motivation Theory and Practice.pdf, pp. 15–16]. Prompt praise for a specific helpful action may encourage employees to repeat it through positive reinforcement [ENG3003_L8_Ch 16 Motivation Theory and Practice.pdf, pp. 29 and 31].

**Implementation.** Bock (2015, ch. 10, "Make it easy to spread the love") describes a simple process. Employees selected a colleague, chose Kudos and wrote a short thank-you message. Chen and Barolak (2020, ch. 3, "Incentives and recognition", including note 11) describe Kudos as public acknowledgement and Peer Bonuses as formal recognition with a cash award for bigger contributions. They give the example of a Peer Bonus for answering questions on a mailing list, where the answers helped people beyond the employee's team.

**Outcomes.** Bock (2015, ch. 10, "Make it easy to spread the love") reports that after Google launched gThanks, Kudos use rose by 460% compared with a year earlier and more than 1,000 employees visited the new version each day. This shows uptake. Its effects on collaboration, productivity and engagement still need testing.

In a separate study, Black et al. (2024, abstract and discussion) found that public feeds and leaderboards can make employees feel less appreciated by prompting social comparison. Under equity theory, employees who see colleagues receive more recognition for similar effort may feel unfairly treated and reduce their effort [ENG3003_L8_Ch 16 Motivation Theory and Practice.pdf, pp. 18–20].

### Question 5

Based on your findings for the selected organization, develop an improved gamification strategy. Propose specific changes based on the issues identified in your research, explain how they would better support organisational goals and culture, and suggest how their effectiveness could be measured.

Google could test four changes to gThanks.

1. Let employees choose private, team or public recognition. A private option would reduce exposure to the comparisons associated with public feeds (Black et al., 2024, abstract and discussion).

1. Add tags for mentoring and knowledge sharing. These tags would make it easier to recognise contributions that Google encourages in its engineering teams (Chen & Barolak, 2020, ch. 3, "Incentives and recognition").

1. Add a category for cross-team help. Ask employees to describe what the colleague did and how it helped the other team. This would recognise contributions like the answers shared on the mailing list in Question 4.

1. Avoid competitive leaderboards to limit the pressure to compare how much recognition employees receive (Black et al., 2024, abstract and discussion).

Google should test the changes for eight weeks with selected teams while similar teams use the current process. To establish a baseline and measure change, survey both groups before and after the pilot. Ask how often employees helped someone on another team and how fair they found recognition in their role. Track the share of employees recognised at least once as a measure of reach.

Google should use the baseline to set an improvement target for cross-team helping before the pilot. Extend the changes to more teams if helping meets the target relative to the comparison teams and fairness ratings stay at or above baseline in each role. If either condition falls short, revise the design and retest. This follows the control process of setting standards, measuring performance, comparing results and taking corrective action [ENG3003_L6_Ch 9 Fundamentals of Control.pdf, pp. 14–18].

## Conclusion1

This report identifies two interconnected lessons regarding gamification in engineering management. Initially, the behaviors that gamification evaluates, promotes, and rewards have the potential to impact organizational culture. The Case F examples indicate that games may facilitate learning, interpersonal connection, and social responsibility. Nevertheless, Disney's productivity-tracking example demonstrates that employees may perceive gamification as a form of pressure rather than encouragement. Therefore, its cultural influence is contingent upon the design, implementation, alignment with the intended values, and employee.

Second, effectiveness cannot be demonstrated solely through participation. According to the Google analysis, gThanks has experienced an increase in usage and offers a convenient mechanism for peer recognition. Nevertheless, the usage evidence examined in this report fails to demonstrate enhancements in productivity, engagement, or collaboration. Rather than being treated as assumed outcomes at Google, popularity, social comparison, and job visibility should be regarded as potential risks that necessitate investigation.

The proposed enhancements are designed to enhance the significance, inclusivity, and alignment of recognition with the organization's objectives. In a broader sense, engineering managers should consult with employees to verify that the behaviors that are rewarded are consistent with the values that are being advocated. In addition, they should evaluate employee experience and work outcomes and pilot changes, rather than relying solely on activity counts. This encompasses the assessment of whether employees are more inclined to assist one another and whether contributions made by employees in various roles are appropriately acknowledged.

The primary takeaway is that gamification is a management tool, not a replacement for effective management. Its value should be assessed based on whether it promotes the desired behaviors and significant results without jeopardizing employee well-being, trust, or fairness.
