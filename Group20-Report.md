## Abstract/executive summary

This report explores how gamification impacts organizational culture. Gamification incorporates game elements such as points, rewards, challenges, and competition into recruitment, training, and daily work. The example in Case F shows that gamification can make work more fun, increase employee engagement and productivity, but poorly designed gamification can also lead to stress, unfairness, and excessive competition.

## Introduction

### 1.1 Case F Background: Gamification in Modern Management

#### 1.1.1 Concept and History of Gamification

Gamification refers to “the use of game design elements in non-game contexts” (Deterding et al., 2011). It applies elements such as points, badges, challenges and rewards to encourage participation and influence behavior. Nick Pelling is widely credited with creating the term in 2002, although its earliest documented industry use was in 2008 and it became widely adopted after 2010 (Sayeed, 2026; Deterding et al., 2011).

#### 1.1.2 Rise in Modern Management

Digital technology has allowed gamification to expand into recruitment, training, performance management and customer engagement. The global gamification market was estimated at US$34.43 billion in 2026, reflecting growing business interest (Review42 Editorial, 2026). Case F includes examples such as Unilever using games in recruitment, IBM supporting remote employees and SAP rewarding sales and environmental activities (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026).

#### 1.1.3 Motivation and Surveillance

Gamification can increase motivation by providing goals, feedback and rewards. However, performance tracking and leaderboards may also create pressure and excessive monitoring. Disney employees described their productivity-tracking system as an “electronic whip,” showing that gamification can motivate employees or become a form of control depending on its design (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026).

### 1.2 Key Engineering Management Issues

The main issue is aligning game objectives with organizational goals. Employees may focus on earning points instead of work quality or teamwork if rewards are poorly designed. Managers must also balance performance monitoring with employee autonomy, fairness and well-being.Gamification should therefore be evaluated through work quality, engagement, teamwork and employee satisfaction, rather than productivity alone. It should support effective management and organizational culture instead of replacing good management.

### Question 1

Analyze how gamification could shape an organization’s culture. Explain which type(s) of culture may be strengthened, and why? Using evidence from the case.

Gamification may strengthen enterprising and social cultures by encouraging behaviors that become shared expectations at work. Organizational culture consists of shared beliefs and values that guide behavior [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. When games regularly reward certain behaviors or make them part of everyday work, they show employees what the organization values and how it expects them to act.

Gamification can strengthen an enterprising culture by encouraging competition and creative problem solving. Both are key features of an enterprising culture [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. Case F explains that rewards and competition can make routine tasks more interesting, while solving problems in games can help employees become more creative and persistent [Case F, p. 2]. Regular challenges and recognition for achievements may encourage employees to look for better solutions and improve their performance. Over time, creativity and achievement may become shared workplace values.

Gamification can also strengthen a social culture by giving colleagues more chances to interact. Social culture emphasizes collaboration and trust [ENG3003_L6_Ch 12 Organizational Change and Development.pdf, p. 4]. IBM uses games to help employees who work from home or travel connect with colleagues and stay engaged [Case F, p. 2]. These interactions may help employees build relationships and feel part of the organization, making it easier for them to work together and support one another. Whether trust or collaboration increased remains unclear from the case.

Gamification can also reinforce values such as learning and social responsibility. Marriott and Siemens use games for employee training [Case F, p. 1]. Using games for training may help employees see learning as a regular part of their work. SAP awards points for environmental activities such as carpooling [Case F, p. 2]. Awarding points for these activities shows employees that the organization values environmental responsibility.

Gamification may weaken a social culture when employees feel pressured to compete. Disney's laundry workers felt pushed to compete even when they did not want to and described productivity tracking as an "electronic whip" [Case F, p. 3]. This pressure may weaken trust in management and lead employees to put individual results ahead of working together.

The practices that are manifested above are detailed in Case F. The lecture's distinction between observable and core culture informs analytical interpretations of their connections to underlying values. These examples demonstrate the potential for gamification to both reflect and reinforce organizational priorities. SAP's points acknowledge environmentally responsible behavior, whereas Unilever's assessment criteria specify which competencies are valued. IBM's shared activities offer an alternative mechanism: opportunities for interaction may foster relationships rather than individual competition. Consequently, cultural messages are conveyed through the activities and assessments that organizations provide, in addition to the rewards they offer. ### Question 3

Positive and Negative Effects of Gamification on Workplace Culture

#### 3.1 Workplace Example

Case F presents several workplace applications of gamification. For example, Unilever uses short digital games to assess applicants’ problem-solving skills, personality and communication style. The results help the company decide which applicants should move to the next recruitment stage (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026; Stephan & Erickson, 2017). However, the effects of gamification are not limited to recruitment. When games are used with existing employees, their design can influence whether colleagues communicate, cooperate or compete with one another.

#### 3.2 Positive Effects on Workplace Culture

IBM uses games to help employees who work remotely or travel frequently stay connected and engaged with their colleagues (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026). This additional contact may help employees feel that they are part of a team and make it easier for them to communicate or ask colleagues for assistance.

This practice may strengthen a social culture, which emphasizes collaboration and trust between employees (Lee, 2026b, slide 4). When gamification creates opportunities for interaction, it can support employee engagement and encourage colleagues to help one another. Nevertheless, Case F does not directly measure whether IBM’s system improved teamwork or trust. These outcomes should therefore be treated as reasonable possible effects rather than confirmed results.

#### 3.3 Negative Effects on Workplace Culture

Disney demonstrates how gamification may produce negative cultural outcomes. The company tracked the individual productivity of laundry workers, who reported feeling pushed and having little “breathing space.” Some employees described the system as an “electronic whip” because they felt forced to compete even when they did not want to (“Case F: Gamification,” 2026).

Individual productivity tracking may make employees focus on keeping up with colleagues instead of supporting them. A worker may be less willing to stop and help another employee if doing so could cause the worker to fall behind. This could weaken the trust and cooperation associated with a social culture.

The Disney example also shows how gamification may change from a motivational tool into a form of management control. Excessive monitoring and forced competition may create stress, damage workplace relationships and reduce employees’ willingness to cooperate.

#### 3.4 Features Leading to Different Outcomes

The difference between the IBM and Disney examples can be explained by several design features. IBM’s system creates opportunities for employees to interact and remain connected. Disney’s system focuses on measuring individual output and encouraging employees to compete.

Positive cultural outcomes are more likely when gamification:

- Creates meaningful opportunities for employee interaction;
- Encourages cooperation and mutual support;
- Connects game activities with organizational goals; and
- Provides clear feedback without excessive monitoring.

Negative outcomes are more likely when gamification:

- Forces employees to compete;
- Constantly monitors individual productivity;
- Encourages employees to compare their performance; or
- Rewards individual results without considering teamwork.

Therefore, managers should design gamification around the workplace behavior they want to encourage. If cooperation is important, the system should make helping colleagues worthwhile instead of rewarding employees only for individual speed or output.

### Question 4

Select ONE organization (Unilever, SAP, Disney or equivalent) and investigate its use of gamification. Evaluate its effectiveness in terms of its purpose, design, implementation, and outcomes, using evidence from multiple external sources.

Google is a great example of a company that has applied gamification to its system. At Google, there is a system called gThanks, an internal peer-recognition system where employees can thank colleagues through Kudos and give stronger recognition through rewards such as Peer Bonuses. Its purpose is to encourage appreciation, collaboration, and helpful behavior by allowing employees, not only managers, to recognize valuable contributions (Bock, 2015).

This purpose aligns with Herzberg’s two-factor theory. The lecture identifies recognition as a satisfier that can increase job satisfaction and motivation. A meaningful word such as thank-you can explain why an employee’s contribution matters. However, recognition cannot compensate for poor working conditions or unfair workloads, which the lecture treats as hygiene factors. [Lecture 8, pages 15–16]

#### Design:

gThanks uses gamification elements such as recognition, rewards, immediate feedback and social visibility. Its main strength is simplicity: employees select a colleague, write a short thank-you message, and send recognition. This low-friction design fits Google’s culture of employee autonomy and openness. Laszlo Bock, Google’s former Head of People Operations, describes gThanks to make peer recognition quick and easy (Bock, 2015).

Using the lecture’s positive reinforcement theory, appreciation following a helpful action may encourage employees to repeat it. The design is strongest when recognition identifies a specific contribution and follows it promptly. Nevertheless, popularity could affect those who receive recognition. Under equity theory, employees who contribute equally but receive fewer rewards may perceive unfairness and reduce their effort. These are design risks, rather than proven outcomes at Google. [Lecture 8, pages 18–20 and 28–31]

#### Implementation:

Recognition is integrated into everyday work rather than limited to formal performance reviews. Employees can recognize contributions that managers may not directly observe. And peer Bonuses are subject to light managerial review and rules that limit reciprocity and manager-to-subordinate awards (Bonusly, 2023; Compt, 2023).

#### Outcomes:

The system achieved strong adoption. After Google simplified gThanks, Kudos usage increased by 460%, with more than 1,000 employees using it each day (Bock, 2015). However, high usage does not necessarily prove improved productivity or engagement. Research also shows that highly public peer-recognition systems can create social comparison, causing some employees to feel less appreciated (Black et al., 2024; Wang, 2023). Therefore, while gThanks successfully increased the volume of recognition and fits Google’s culture of openness, evidence that it strengthened collaboration remains limited, and fairness risks persist.

Overall, gThanks is effective at raising recognition and supporting Google’s collaborative culture but slightly limited by fairness and excessive competition as potential weaknesses.

### Question 5

Based on your findings for the selected organization, develop an improved gamification strategy. Propose specific changes based on the issues identified in your research, explain how they would better support organizational goals and culture, and suggest how their effectiveness could be measured.

Based on the limitations identified in Question 4, limited evidence of improved collaboration and the risk of social comparison, Google could improve gThanks through four changes:

1. Make recognition visibility optional. Employees could choose private, team, or public recognition. This would reduce negative social comparison while maintaining appreciation.
2. Add tags for mentoring and knowledge sharing. These tags would make it easier to recognize contributions that Google encourages in its engineering teams (Chen & Barolak, 2020, ch. 3, "Incentives and recognition").
3. Reward cross-team collaboration. Give additional recognition for helping employees outside one's immediate team. This would discourage recognition from becoming limited to close colleagues.
4. Avoid competitive leaderboards to limit the pressure to compare how much recognition employees receive (Black et al., 2024, abstract and discussion).

To measure the effectiveness, Google could pilot an improved version of gThanks.

#### The pilot design:

- Baseline period 6 weeks: collect current metrics under the existing gThanks system.
- Pilot period 12 weeks: introduce the improved version.
- Comparison teams: parallel teams continue using the current system during the pilot period.

#### The key measures:

- Participation rate (percentage of employees who give or receive recognition at least once).
- Number of unique employees recognized.
- Share of recognition that is cross-team.
- Employee survey items on perceived fairness of recognition and feeling appreciated.
- Self-reported and peer-reported helping behavior, especially cross-team.

Success would be indicated by higher cross-team recognition, stable or improved fairness scores, and no decline in overall participation relative to comparison teams. And if the pilot shows positive results on fairness and cross-team helping without reducing usage, the changes could be rolled out more widely. In the end, this approach can turn the recommendations into a testable improvement to the current system.

## Conclusion

This report identifies two interconnected lessons regarding gamification in engineering management. Initially, the behaviors that gamification evaluates, promotes, and rewards have the potential to impact organizational culture. The Case F examples indicate that games may facilitate learning, interpersonal connection, and social responsibility. Nevertheless, Disney's productivity-tracking example demonstrates that employees may perceive gamification as a form of pressure rather than encouragement. Therefore, its cultural influence is contingent upon the design, implementation, alignment with the intended values, and employee.

Second, effectiveness cannot be demonstrated solely through participation. According to the Google analysis, gThanks has experienced an increase in usage and offers a convenient mechanism for peer recognition. Nevertheless, the usage evidence examined in this report fails to demonstrate enhancements in productivity, engagement, or collaboration. Rather than being treated as assumed outcomes at Google, popularity, social comparison, and job visibility should be regarded as potential risks that necessitate investigation.

The proposed enhancements are designed to enhance the significance, inclusivity, and alignment of recognition with the organization's objectives. In a broader sense, engineering managers should consult with employees to verify that the behaviors that are rewarded are consistent with the values that are being advocated. In addition, they should evaluate employee experience and work outcomes and pilot changes, rather than relying solely on activity counts. This encompasses the assessment of whether employees are more inclined to assist one another and whether contributions made by employees in various roles are appropriately acknowledged.

The primary takeaway is that gamification is a management tool, not a replacement for effective management. Its value should be assessed based on whether it promotes the desired behaviors and significant results without jeopardizing employee well-being, trust, or fairness.
